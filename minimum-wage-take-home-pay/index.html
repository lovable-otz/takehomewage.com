Minimum Wage Take-Home Pay Calculator

On the £12.71 minimum wage at 37.5 hours a week for 52 weeks, minimum wage take home pay is £21,364.44 a year, about £1,780 a month, from gross pay of £24,784.50. That is after £2,442.90 Income Tax and £977.16 National Insurance, with no pension and the standard 1257L tax code.

Two things stand out. At 20 hours a week you keep almost all of it: gross pay is only £648.40 above the Personal Allowance, so the whole year costs £181.55 in tax and National Insurance combined. At 40 hours the deductions come to £3,882.70, roughly 14.7% of gross.

The gap between 37.5 and 40 hours is £1,652.30 gross but only £1,189.66 net, because every extra pound over £12,570 is taxed at 20% and charged 8% National Insurance, a combined 28% marginal rate.

These are annual figures. Your payslip works on each pay period instead, so a week with overtime or a month with five paydays can look different even though the year comes out the same. Weekly take-home works out at £250.71 (20 hours), £342.22 (30 hours), £410.85 (37.5 hours) and £433.73 (40 hours). Student loan repayments, salary sacrifice, an emergency tax code and unpaid weeks are all excluded here, and each will change what actually lands in your account.

How tax and NI are worked out

Income Tax and National Insurance are worked out separately, on different bases, and both start at £12,570 a year.

Income Tax: the Personal Allowance is £12,570, so the first £12,570 of your pay is untaxed. Anything from £12,571 to £50,270 is taxed at 20%, £50,271 to £125,140 at 40%, and above £125,140 at 45%. Above £100,000 the Personal Allowance itself shrinks by £1 for every £2 of income. Nobody on the minimum wage gets near those upper bands: 40 hours a week at £12.71 is £26,436.80, well inside the basic rate.

National Insurance: employees pay 8% on earnings between the £12,570 primary threshold and the £50,270 upper earnings limit, then 2% above that. National Insurance is charged on your gross pay, and a pension contribution does not reduce it under a normal auto-enrolment scheme.

Worked through at 37.5 hours on £12.71:

  • Gross pay: £12.71 × 37.5 × 52 = £24,784.50
  • Taxable above the allowance: £24,784.50 − £12,570 = £12,214.50
  • Income Tax: £12,214.50 × 20% = £2,442.90
  • National Insurance: £12,214.50 × 8% = £977.16
  • Take-home: £21,364.44, or £1,780.37 a month

The calculator assumes tax code 1257L, which is the code for one job with the full Personal Allowance. If you have two jobs, a company benefit or an underpayment being collected, your code will differ and so will the tax. Check the code on your payslip against your HMRC personal tax account before you rely on any figure here.

Pension auto-enrolment

If you are aged 22 to State Pension age, earn at least £10,000 a year and ordinarily work in the UK, your employer must enrol you into a workplace pension automatically. On the minimum wage that threshold matters: at £12.71 an hour you cross £10,000 at about 15.2 hours a week.

The legal minimum total contribution is 8% of qualifying earnings, of which your employer must put in at least 3% and you put in the rest, normally 5%. Qualifying earnings are the slice of pay between £6,240 and £50,270 a year, not your whole salary, which is why the cost is lower than 5% of gross sounds.

At 37.5 hours the £927.23 contribution only reduces take-home by £741.78, because the contribution comes off before Income Tax and saves £185.45 of tax at 20%. Your employer adds at least 3% of qualifying earnings on top, £556.34 at 37.5 hours, which is money you do not get if you opt out.

You can opt out, and within the first month your contributions are refunded. Some schemes instead work on your full pay or use salary sacrifice, which also cuts National Insurance; check your scheme’s rules, because the calculator uses the qualifying earnings method that most auto-enrolment schemes follow.

Scotland note

The calculator uses England, Wales and Northern Ireland Income Tax bands. If you live in Scotland the minimum wage is identical, because it is set UK-wide, but Income Tax is devolved and the bands are different, so the take-home figure on this page will be slightly out for you.

Scottish Income Tax bands run to six bands rather than three: a starter rate of 19% from £12,571 to £16,537, a basic rate of 20% to £29,526, an intermediate rate of 21% to £43,662, a higher rate of 42% to £75,000, an advanced rate of 45% to £125,140 and a top rate of 48% above that. The £12,570 Personal Allowance is the same, and it is still withdrawn above £100,000.

For a minimum wage worker the difference is small and in your favour. At 37.5 hours on £12.71, gross pay of £24,784.50 gives £3,967 taxed at the 19% starter rate (£753.73) and £8,247.50 taxed at 20% (£1,649.50), a total of £2,403.23. The England, Wales and Northern Ireland figure is £2,442.90, so a Scottish taxpayer pays £39.67 less over the year, about £3.31 a month.

National Insurance is not devolved, so the 8% and 2% rates and the £12,570 and £50,270 thresholds apply the same way across the whole UK. The pension and qualifying earnings figures are UK-wide too.

Whether you count as a Scottish taxpayer depends on where your main home is for most of the tax year, not where your employer is based. HMRC decides it and applies an S prefix to your tax code, so check whether your payslip shows something like S1257L. Scottish bands are a planned addition to this calculator; until then, use the tick box to see the warning and treat the tax line as the rest-of-UK figure.
